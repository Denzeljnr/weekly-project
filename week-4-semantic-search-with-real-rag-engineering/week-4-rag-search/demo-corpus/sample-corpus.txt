The History and Practice of Coffee Roasting

Coffee roasting is the process that transforms raw, green coffee beans into the fragrant, brown beans used to brew coffee. Before roasting, green coffee beans are dense, grassy-smelling, and largely inert in flavor. The roasting process triggers a cascade of chemical reactions, most notably the Maillard reaction and caramelization, that develop the hundreds of aromatic compounds responsible for coffee's flavor.

Roasting begins when green beans are loaded into a heated drum or fluid-bed roaster, typically starting at temperatures between 180 and 240 degrees Celsius. As the beans absorb heat, they pass through several distinct phases: drying, where residual moisture evaporates and the beans turn from green to yellow; the "first crack," an audible popping sound caused by steam pressure rupturing the bean's cell structure; and, for darker roasts, a "second crack," where the bean's oils begin to migrate to the surface.

Light roasts are typically stopped shortly after first crack, preserving more of the bean's original acidity and origin characteristics. Medium roasts extend slightly further, balancing acidity with body. Dark roasts continue past second crack, producing bold, smoky flavors but sacrificing much of the nuanced flavor that distinguishes one coffee's origin from another. This is part of why specialty coffee roasters tend to favor lighter roast profiles: darker roasting tends to homogenize flavor across different origins.

The history of coffee roasting stretches back centuries. Coffee is believed to have originated in the Kaffa region of Ethiopia, where legend attributes its discovery to a goatherd named Kaldi, who noticed his goats became unusually energetic after eating berries from a certain shrub. From Ethiopia, coffee cultivation and consumption spread to the Arabian Peninsula, where it was first roasted and brewed in the 15th century in Yemen. Sufi monasteries used coffee to stay alert during nighttime prayers, and the drink spread through the Islamic world via Mecca and Cairo before reaching Istanbul.

Coffee arrived in Europe in the 17th century, first through Venetian trade routes, and coffeehouses quickly became centers of intellectual and commercial exchange in cities like London, Paris, and Vienna. In England, coffeehouses were sometimes called "penny universities" because for the price of a cup of coffee, one could sit and participate in wide-ranging discussions on politics, science, and business. Lloyd's of London, the insurance market, traces its origins to a coffeehouse where merchants and ship owners gathered to discuss shipping news.

Modern roasting technology has moved far beyond the simple pan-roasting methods of centuries past. Industrial roasters use computer-controlled airflow and temperature profiles to produce highly consistent results at scale, while artisanal roasters often prize manual control, adjusting heat and airflow by feel and experience throughout the roast. Roast profiling software now allows roasters to log and replicate exact temperature curves for a given bean, batch after batch.

Sustainability has also become a major theme in the coffee industry. Because coffee is grown almost exclusively in a narrow equatorial band sometimes called the "bean belt," climate change poses a direct threat to future coffee-growing regions. Rising temperatures and shifting rainfall patterns are pushing some farmers to higher elevations in search of suitable growing conditions, while others are experimenting with more heat-tolerant coffee varieties, including the historically less-prized robusta species and lesser-known wild coffee relatives.

Freshness plays an enormous role in the final flavor of brewed coffee. Roasted coffee begins releasing carbon dioxide immediately after roasting, a process called degassing, and beans are generally considered to be at peak flavor somewhere between four and fourteen days after roasting. Coffee that is ground releases its aromatic compounds far more quickly than whole beans, which is why most quality-focused coffee drinkers grind beans immediately before brewing rather than buying pre-ground coffee.

Brewing method also significantly affects the final cup, independent of the roast itself. Immersion methods, like the French press, extract oils and fine particulates that filtered methods remove, producing a heavier body. Pour-over methods, using a paper filter, produce a cleaner, brighter cup that showcases more of a coffee's acidity and delicate aromatics. Espresso, brewed under high pressure through a very fine grind, concentrates flavor and produces the crema layer prized in espresso-based drinks.

Coffee cupping, the standardized method professionals use to evaluate coffee quality, involves smelling the dry grounds, then the wet grounds after hot water is added, and finally slurping the brewed coffee loudly across the tongue to aerate it and maximize flavor perception. Cuppers score coffee across categories including aroma, acidity, body, flavor, and aftertaste, usually on a 100-point scale, with anything above 80 points generally considered "specialty grade."

Decaffeination is another area with a surprisingly long and technical history. The Swiss Water Process uses only water, temperature, and time to remove caffeine, avoiding chemical solvents entirely, and is popular among organic-certified producers. Other methods use solvents like ethyl acetate or methylene chloride to strip caffeine from green beans before roasting. Because caffeine is water-soluble, all decaffeination methods work by first opening the bean's pores, usually by steaming, and then repeatedly washing the caffeine out.

Coffee's economic footprint is vast. It is one of the most heavily traded commodities in the world, supporting the livelihoods of an estimated 25 million farmers globally, the large majority of whom operate small farms of five hectares or less. Price volatility on commodity markets can have an outsized effect on these smallholder farmers, which has driven the growth of direct trade and fair trade certification models intended to provide more stable and equitable pricing.

Looking forward, researchers are exploring lab-grown and cell-cultured coffee as a potential hedge against the risks climate change poses to traditional cultivation. While these products remain far from commercial viability at scale, some startups have already produced small quantities of coffee-flavored compounds without using a single coffee plant, grown instead from plant cell cultures in bioreactors.
